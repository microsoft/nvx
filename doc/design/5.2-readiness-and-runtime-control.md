# 5.2 Readiness and Runtime Control

[Architecture documentation](README.md) >
[5. VM Lifecycle and State](5-vm-lifecycle-and-state.md)

Readiness is an explicit lifecycle boundary, not the observation of arbitrary
console text. A cold boot is usable when the guest agents have initialized the
required devices and either prepared the one-shot workload or established the
managed control path, and a managed guest's init says so with
[Ready](#524-ready).

## 5.2.1 Control Frontends

Two frontends drive the VM worker: command-line launch and the management
RPC. Both construct the same machine profile, and the worker applies the same
validation, snapshot boundaries, and management exclusion to both. Either
frontend creates, captures, and restores machines, attaches their host
resources, publishes readiness, and reports guest exit. Generic host-initiated
save and pulse save-and-restore operations are unavailable for the microVM:
capture is always guest-requested. `nvx` drives the command-line frontend: it
renders each launch's whole command line and starts OpenVMM with it.

## 5.2.2 Readiness Endpoint

A caller may supply a single-use readiness endpoint for a restore: an
existing Unix socket on Linux or named pipe on Windows. The VMM connects to it
before the worker launches. When the restored VM first starts, the worker
writes one fixed readiness record after every state unit has started, each
device having confirmed its start, and before it releases a restored
processor. For a gated restore, host input is still gated at that point, and
guest repair and its acknowledgement follow the event. A device that fails to
start, or a connection, write, or flush failure, aborts startup, and the
endpoint is never saved in a snapshot.

`nvx` gives every restore that it creates such an endpoint, which it binds
before OpenVMM starts: a socket private to the caller in the VM's directory on
Linux, or a fresh named pipe with an owner-only access control list on
Windows. It accepts the record only from the VM's held process, which it
verifies through the socket's original peer on Linux and the pipe client's
process ID on Windows, and only byte for byte as OpenVMM writes it:
`OPENVMM_RESTORE_READY_V1` and a newline.

## 5.2.3 Management Exclusion

While the worker holds the snapshot stop guard, its central dispatcher
immediately rejects management operations that could disturb the boundary,
including memory writes, resume or reset, device changes, and state dumps,
rather than queueing them. Snapshot quiesce, rollback, boundary release, and
memory reads remain available. The exclusion applies at the worker boundary
that every management frontend shares.

## 5.2.4 Ready

A managed guest's init reports Ready on the control session once it can take
work: after a cold boot, once it has built its space, and after a restore,
once its activation has applied the restore's activation record, in answer to
the request that carries the record. Ready belongs to the protocol that the
caller speaks with its guest inside the control session, not to OpenVMM, whose
readiness record only says that the restored VM started. The host sends the
mapping table, and starts a workload, only after Ready.

What a layered root's Ready reports about its activation is a public contract
of `nvx`, `GuestRestoreReady`, with a canonical encoding: nothing more for a
cold boot, and for a restore, the transaction ID of its activation record, which
the caller picks for each restore and keeps across the restore's retries, so
that Ready ties to the record of that restore. A restore that `nvx`'s
`RestoreMachine` orders ends at a Ready that echoes its transaction, and fails
at any other. NVX's own control protocol, NVXC, private to `nvx`, carries Ready
with its protocol version, which NVX's host checks.

## 5.2.5 The Host's Mapping Table

When a VM exports host paths, its host hands the guest a mapping table: the
ordered binds of the export, each with a source relative to the guest's mount
of the export, an absolute target in the guest, and its access, read-only or
read-write. `nvx` derives the table from the caller's export, in the order of
its binds, and encodes it in `MAPS` parts. The host announces how many
mappings the table holds before the guest is ready: at a cold boot with the
`nvx_maps=` boot token that `nvx` renders, and after a restore in the
activation record. Once the guest is ready, the host sends the table in parts
of consecutive entries, in the caller's protocol, and sends none of an empty
table.

The guest keeps its account of the table in the public `MappingTable` of
`nvx`, which any init uses. It refuses as a whole, and changes nothing for, a
part that does not decode, holds no entry, does not continue the table, ends
past the announced count, or names the export's root itself. It hands the init
each other part's mappings in order. A mapping that fails latches it: the
mappings before it stay, and the table refuses every later part. It is
complete once every announced mapping is applied and none failed, and an init
starts no workload before then. Applying a mapping is the init's own: in NVX's
reference space, which nvx-init implements and which is private to `nvx`, a
mapper binds each mapping beneath the descriptors of the root's mounts, never
by path. The
[guest-agent guidelines](../guest-agent-guidelines.md#the-hosts-mapping-table)
give an init's part of the table's contract.

## 5.2.6 Lifecycle Operations

As Table 5.2 shows, `nvx` gives its caller five operations on a VM's
lifecycle, in the caller's private VM directory.

**Table 5.2.** A VM's lifecycle operations in `nvx`.

| Operation | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Create | Checks a cold boot, a capture, or a restore, and creates the launch's log; for a restore, checks the snapshot, links its files, and binds the restore-ready endpoint. It starts no process |
| Spawn | Starts OpenVMM detached, with the capability on its standard input, and returns its verified process |
| Verify and reattach | Checks a saved VM's runtime identity and process, then connects to its verified control endpoint and authenticates with its saved capability |
| Stop | Waits for the VM to end after the caller's own request, then terminates only its verified process, and reports an exit only once it is confirmed |
| Finish a capture | Checks the outcome of an exited capture, and writes its snapshot's key and description |

A caller may also take a created launch's complete command, with its opened
log and inherited resources, and start OpenVMM with a launcher of its own.
Everything around these operations is the caller's: where its VM directories
live and for how long, what it saves to find a VM again, how it serializes its
own operations on one VM, and its protocol with the guest, which also ends the
VM gracefully.

NVX's command-line interface (CLI) is one such caller. Its `sandbox` command
keeps a persistent sandbox in a store of its own, with five operations of its
own, provision, start, exec, stop, and deprovision, which fail closed and take
effect one at a time per sandbox, under its store's lock, while executions run
beside them. It journals each launch before its spawn, reconciles VMM
processes with its records, and recovers interrupted launches. Teardown leaves
no VMM process, control endpoint, capability, or runtime record, while
supplied layers and scratch survive. A sandbox's writes to its root therefore
persist from one start to the next on scratch, but not when its overlay's
upper is in guest memory, which each start begins empty.

## 5.2.7 Authority

Runtime control is divided by authority:

- The caller requests VM lifecycle transitions through `nvx`, which reaches
  only the VM's verified process and keeps no lifecycle lock, so the caller
  serializes its own transitions of one VM.
- The VMM serializes transitions that affect machine state.
- The guest agents own workload start, stop, supervision, and capture
  preparation.
- The workload cannot invoke host management interfaces directly.

Operations that could disturb a capture or restore boundary are rejected
while that boundary is active rather than queued for later execution.
